3. **Water or animal damage:** Show wilting after missed irrigation, or photographed grazing damage. The system avoids calling either case a fungal disease.

## The distinctive feature: an investigation, not a diagnosis label

Suppose leaves are yellow. The app should ask targeted questions:

1. Is yellowing on **older leaves, younger leaves, or scattered plants**?
2. Is the problem throughout the field or concentrated in a **low-lying patch**?
3. When was the field last irrigated, and has it rained heavily?
4. Is there a recent soil test? Is its sample from this field?
5. Are insects or root problems visible?

Then it returns a **ranked investigation**:

> **Most useful next check:** inspect roots and soil moisture in the affected patch.  
> **Why:** symptoms are clustered in a low area after heavy rain.  
> **Also possible:** nutrient deficiency; the soil report is too old to resolve it.  
> **Current conclusion:** insufficient evidence to recommend a treatment.

That is a much better demonstration of agricultural reasoning than a screen saying “Disease: 94%.”

## The core farmer problem

A farmer may have a soil report in one place, a weather alert elsewhere, photos on a phone, and irrigation or pesticide details in memory. When a problem appears, nobody has the complete history of **this field, this crop, this season**.

The app’s value is to connect those records, spot changes, ask for the most useful missing observation, and track whether an action worked.

## Inputs worth collecting

The key design principle is **low effort for the farmer**. Capture location, time, and weather automatically where possible. Ask for manual input only when it changes a decision.

## Features I would actually build

### 1. Field timeline

One view shows sowing, rainfall, irrigation, fertiliser, pest sightings, symptoms, and actions in date order. A chat tool can discuss a photo; the app can show that wilting began **after three missed irrigations** or that damage is spreading from one corner of a specific field.

### 2. Guided scouting route

The app asks the farmer to inspect a few points across the field and capture consistent photos and counts. This avoids diagnosing an entire field from its worst-looking leaf. Geotagged observations become a map of **where, how much, and whether it is spreading**.

### 3. Evidence-based problem investigation

The output should be:

- **Possible causes**, ranked with supporting and conflicting evidence.
- **What is missing**, such as an underside photo, root check, soil moisture reading, or pest count.
- **The next useful action**, including when expert or laboratory confirmation is appropriate.

It should allow **multiple simultaneous causes**. A field can have both nutrient stress and insect damage.

### 4. Time-sensitive alerts

Alerts should relate to the farmer’s field and crop stage: “Scout the low-lying patch after heavy rain,” or “Repeat the pest count at the marked locations tomorrow.” Avoid generic daily notifications.

### 5. Action and outcome tracking

The farmer records what they did, cost, and whether the affected area improved. This turns advice into a testable decision. The app can later show which problems recurred and which interventions had an observed benefit, without claiming that correlation proves causation.

### 6. Expert handoff

Generate a compact case file for an agronomist or local extension worker: field location, crop stage, images, relevant soil and water results, weather, operations, and the unanswered question. That is far more useful than forwarding a chatbot conversation.
